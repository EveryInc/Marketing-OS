# Canon

Every's reference encyclopedia of the thinkers, practitioners, and researchers whose work
underpins the Marketing OS. Organized by domain. Douglas's philosophical groundings
(semiotics, ways of seeing, design as art, style, memetics, positioning, behavioral
economics, the spectacle) come first in all work; `groundings.md` turns them into
questions and tests. Each entry provides a name, role, core
contribution, and guidance on when to invoke that thinker's lens.

---

## Effectiveness

### Les Binet
**Role**: Effectiveness strategist; head of effectiveness at adam&eveDDB.
**Contribution**:
With Peter Field, analyzed the IPA Databank of effectiveness case studies to show that brand building and sales activation work on different timescales. Their work argues most brands should weight spend toward long-term brand building (roughly 60/40, varying by category). Emotional, fame-driven campaigns tend to outperform rational ones on long-term business results.
**When to invoke**: When balancing brand-building vs. activation spend, when arguing for long-term investment, when applying the 60/40 framework or its category-specific variants.

### Peter Field
**Role**: Marketing effectiveness consultant; co-author of the IPA effectiveness research.
**Contribution**:
Co-authored The Long and the Short of It with Les Binet, using decades of IPA Databank cases. Showed that emotional creative and fame effects drive more profit growth over time than short-term rational messaging. Documented the risk of over-investing in short-term activation and the decline in creative effectiveness when it happens.
**When to invoke**: When grounding a strategy in IPA databank evidence, when building the case for emotional creative, when evaluating campaign effectiveness criteria.

### Byron Sharp
**Role**: Professor of marketing science; director of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute.
**Contribution**:
Author of How Brands Grow (2010). Argues brands grow mainly by increasing penetration (more buyers) rather than loyalty, and that growth depends on mental availability (being easy to recall in buying situations) and physical availability (being easy to buy). Distinctiveness matters more than differentiation for memory; see `semiotics-heuristics.md` for where Every departs from his generalizations.
**When to invoke**: When applying mental/physical availability, when challenging loyalty-first strategies, when designing for reach and penetration over frequency.

### Andrew Ehrenberg
**Role**: Statistician and marketing scientist; founder of the Ehrenberg-Bass tradition.
**Contribution**:
Founded the empirical tradition behind the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute. Showed that repeat-buying follows stable statistical patterns (the NBD-Dirichlet), and that brands in a category share customers roughly in line with their size (duplication of purchase). His work is why Every treats loyalty as a result of reach and memory, not a separate lever.
**When to invoke**: When grounding claims in repeat-purchase patterns, when arguing against segmentation orthodoxy, when applying the NBD-Dirichlet model.

### Jenni Romaniuk
**Role**: Research professor at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute; authority on distinctive assets and CEPs.
**Contribution**:
Author of Building Distinctive Brand Assets and co-author of How Brands Grow Part 2. Defined distinctive assets as non-name cues (colors, shapes, characters, sounds) measured on fame and uniqueness. Developed category entry points: the situations and needs that should bring a brand to mind.
**When to invoke**: When building or auditing distinctive brand assets, when mapping category entry points, when operationalizing mental availability.

## Creative

### David Ogilvy
**Role**: Founder of Ogilvy & Mather; pioneer of research-informed creative.
**Contribution**:
Founder of Ogilvy & Mather and author of Confessions of an Advertising Man (1963) and Ogilvy on Advertising (1983). Insisted on research before creative, a big idea people remember, long-term brand image, and respect for the reader's intelligence. Showed that long, specific, fact-rich copy can be elegant and still sell.
**When to invoke**: When grounding creative in direct-response discipline, when writing long-form copy, when the work needs a blend of elegance and salesmanship.

### Bill Bernbach
**Role**: Co-founder of DDB; architect of the Creative Revolution.
**Contribution**:
Co-founder of DDB, whose Volkswagen and Avis work led the Creative Revolution of the 1960s. Argued that persuasion is an art, that execution can be the idea, and that honest, self-aware copy earns more trust than claims. Paired copywriter and art director as one team.
**When to invoke**: When the work needs to break convention, when arguing for creative as strategy, when the brief calls for irreverence grounded in human truth.

### Dan Wieden
**Role**: Co-founder of Wieden+Kennedy; architect of brand voice as cultural artifact.
**Contribution**:
Co-founder of Wieden+Kennedy and writer of "Just Do It" (1988). Built brand voice as a cultural artifact that people adopt as their own words. Showed that a brand can stand for an attitude larger than its product and hold it for decades.
**When to invoke**: When building brand voice that transcends advertising, when the work needs to feel like culture not commerce, when writing taglines or manifestos.

### Jeff Goodby
**Role**: Co-founder of Goodby Silverstein & Partners; storyteller and craft maximalist.
**Contribution**:
Co-founder of Goodby Silverstein & Partners, behind "got milk?" (1993). Used humor, narrative, and deprivation framing to make familiar products feel urgent. Treats craft and storytelling as the strategy, not decoration on it.
**When to invoke**: When the work demands narrative craft, when humor is a strategic tool, when the brief needs an unexpected entry point.

### Lee Clow
**Role**: Chairman of TBWA\Media Arts Lab; creative force behind Apple's brand identity.
**Contribution**:
Long-time creative director of Chiat/Day and TBWA\Media Arts Lab, behind Apple's "1984" and "Think Different." Made simplicity and a single platform idea the core of iconic brands. Treated the brand itself as the medium.
**When to invoke**: When simplicity is the strategy, when building iconic brand platforms, when the work needs to feel inevitable rather than clever.

### Phyllis Robinson
**Role**: First female copy chief at a major agency (DDB); creative pioneer.
**Contribution**:
First copy chief at Doyle Dane Bernbach. Wrote in natural, conversational language that treated readers as intelligent equals (Levy's rye bread, early Polaroid work). Showed that warmth and plain speech can carry a brand as well as wit.
**When to invoke**: When the work needs conversational warmth, when writing copy that respects the reader's intelligence, when grounding creative in natural speech.

### Mary Wells Lawrence
**Role**: Founder of Wells Rich Greene; first female CEO of a NYSE-listed company.
**Contribution**:
Founder of Wells Rich Greene and the first woman to be CEO of a company listed on the NYSE. Led "I ♥ NY" and Braniff's "End of the Plain Plane," changing products, uniforms, and environments rather than only ads. Proved advertising can lead a total brand transformation.
**When to invoke**: When the brief calls for total brand transformation, when creative must extend beyond advertising into experience, when ambition is the strategy.

### Brian Collins
**Role**: Chief creative officer of COLLINS; brand designer and design strategist.
**Contribution**:
Founder of COLLINS, the brand design firm (Spotify, Dropbox, Instacart identity work). Treats brand identity as a living system that behaves across products, not a fixed logo. Joins design craft with strategy so that identity carries meaning, not just recognition.
**When to invoke**: When brand identity is the deliverable, when design thinking meets brand strategy, when the work needs to operate at the intersection of beauty and systems.

## Brand Theory

### David Aaker
**Role**: Professor emeritus at Haas School of Business; father of modern brand management.
**Contribution**:
Author of Managing Brand Equity (1991) and Building Strong Brands (1996). Defined brand equity as awareness, associations, perceived quality, loyalty, and proprietary assets, and the brand identity system of core and extended identity seen as product, organization, person, and symbol. Every's Brand Book uses his structure.
**When to invoke**: When building a brand identity system, when evaluating brand equity dimensions, when structuring a brand architecture or portfolio strategy.

### Kevin Lane Keller
**Role**: Professor of marketing at Dartmouth; architect of the CBBE pyramid.
**Contribution**:
Created customer-based brand equity (CBBE) and the brand resonance pyramid: salience, performance and imagery, judgments and feelings, then resonance. Treats equity as what customers know and feel, measured against a target audience. See `marketing-science/brand-equity/references/keller.md`.
**When to invoke**: When building brand equity from the ground up, when diagnosing where a brand is stuck in the equity pyramid, when the task involves brand resonance or salience.

### Jean-Noël Kapferer
**Role**: Professor of marketing strategy; authority on brand identity and luxury branding.
**Contribution**:
Created the brand identity prism: physique, personality, culture, relationship, reflection, and self-image. Separates what the brand sends from how the receiver sees themselves through it. Authority on luxury brand strategy and the logic of rarity.
**When to invoke**: When the brand identity prism is the right diagnostic, when working on premium or luxury positioning, when brand and product identity need separation.

### Wally Olins
**Role**: Co-founder of Wolff Olins; pioneer of corporate identity and nation branding.
**Contribution**:
Co-founder of Wolff Olins and author of Corporate Identity (1989). Showed that identity expresses what an organization is and does across products, environments, communication, and behavior. Extended branding to nations and institutions.
**When to invoke**: When brand is an organizational strategy problem, when identity must unify across complex entities, when brand extends beyond marketing into culture and operations.

### Marty Neumeier
**Role**: Author and brand strategist; bridge between design thinking and business strategy.
**Contribution**:
Author of The Brand Gap (2003) and Zag (2006). Defines a brand as a person's gut feeling about a product, and the brand gap as the distance between strategy and creative. Zag argues for radical differentiation when the market converges.
**When to invoke**: When translating brand strategy into plain language, when the audience is cross-functional, when the task requires a "brand gap" diagnosis between strategy and creative.

## Behavioral

### Rory Sutherland
**Role**: Vice chairman of Ogilvy UK; practitioner of behavioral economics in marketing.
**Contribution**:
Vice chairman of Ogilvy UK and author of Alchemy (2019). Argues that psychological value can matter as much as material value, and that the logical answer is often not the most effective one. Finds counterintuitive levers in framing, signaling, and perception. See `method.md` for the rules Every uses.
**When to invoke**: When reframing a problem through behavioral lenses, when psychological value can substitute for material value, when the work needs a counterintuitive insight.

### Daniel Kahneman
**Role**: Nobel laureate in economics; co-discoverer of cognitive biases and dual-process theory.
**Contribution**:
Nobel laureate and author of Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011). With Amos Tversky, showed that people rely on fast intuitive judgments (System 1) prone to predictable biases, and developed prospect theory (losses loom larger than gains). The basis for framing and availability in Every's work.
**When to invoke**: When System 1 processing is the strategic lever, when framing effects matter, when the work needs to account for how people actually decide, not how they say they decide.

### Dan Ariely
**Role**: Professor of psychology and behavioral economics; researcher in predictable irrationality.
**Contribution**:
Author of Predictably Irrational (2008). Studied anchoring, the zero-price effect, decoy options, and the clash between social norms and market norms. Some of his studies have faced data-integrity questions, so cite the established effects, not single findings.
**When to invoke**: When pricing, anchoring, or choice architecture is central to the task, when designing offers or bundles, when the work needs to account for irrational but predictable behavior.

### Robert Cialdini
**Role**: Regents' professor of psychology; codifier of the principles of persuasion.
**Contribution**:
Author of Influence (1984). Codified reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, and scarcity, later adding unity. Every uses them only within the ethics line in `method.md`.
**When to invoke**: When the work involves persuasion mechanics (social proof, scarcity, reciprocity, etc.), when writing CTAs or conversion copy, when designing influence architecture.

### Richard Thaler
**Role**: Nobel laureate in economics; co-architect of nudge theory.
**Contribution**:
Nobel laureate and co-author with Cass Sunstein of Nudge (2008). Showed that choice architecture (defaults, ordering, framing) changes behavior without removing choice. Coined mental accounting and, with Sunstein's later work, the idea of sludge: harmful friction.
**When to invoke**: When designing choice architecture, when defaults and opt-ins are strategic, when applying nudge thinking to product or marketing design.

## Strategy

### Michael Porter
**Role**: Professor at Harvard Business School; architect of competitive strategy.
**Contribution**:
Author of Competitive Strategy (1980) and Competitive Advantage (1985). Defined the five forces and the generic strategies of cost leadership, differentiation, and focus. Argued strategy means choosing a unique position and the trade-offs that protect it.
**When to invoke**: When the task involves competitive positioning, when evaluating cost vs. differentiation, when the strategic question is about where to play and how to win at the industry level.

### Al Ries
**Role**: Marketing strategist; co-originator of positioning theory.
**Contribution**:
Co-author with Jack Trout of Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind (1981) and The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing (1993). Argued that positioning happens in the prospect's mind, that being first beats being better, and that a brand should own a single word. Warned against line extensions that blur the position.
**When to invoke**: When the task is positioning from scratch, when the brand needs to own a word in the mind, when simplicity and focus are the strategic imperatives.

### Jack Trout
**Role**: Marketing strategist; co-originator of positioning theory.
**Contribution**:
Co-originated positioning with Al Ries, first in Advertising Age (1972). Developed repositioning the competition and owning a place on the mental ladder of a category. Later wrote Differentiate or Die (2000) on finding a defensible difference.
**When to invoke**: When the competitive frame needs defining, when differentiation is the problem, when the task involves repositioning against an incumbent.

### Richard Rumelt
**Role**: Professor of strategy at UCLA Anderson; diagnostician of good vs. bad strategy.
**Contribution**:
Author of Good Strategy Bad Strategy (2011). Defines the kernel of strategy as a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and coherent actions. Exposes bad strategy as fluff, goals mistaken for strategy, and failure to face the challenge.
**When to invoke**: When diagnosing whether a strategy is actually a strategy, when the work needs a kernel (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action), when challenging fluffy strategic language.

### Roger Martin
**Role**: Former dean of Rotman School of Management; strategist of integrative thinking.
**Contribution**:
Co-author of Playing to Win (2013) with A.G. Lafley. Frames strategy as an integrated set of choices: winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities, and management systems. Developed integrative thinking for resolving opposing models.
**When to invoke**: When the strategic question is "where to play / how to win," when resolving tensions between opposing models, when the task involves strategy as choice rather than planning.

## Deep History

### Edward Bernays
**Role**: Pioneer of public relations; nephew of Freud; architect of consent engineering.
**Contribution**:
Author of Crystallizing Public Opinion (1923) and Propaganda (1928). Applied psychology to shape public opinion and founded modern public relations. A cautionary lineage: Every studies him for how narrative framing works, not as a model of practice.
**When to invoke**: When understanding the roots of PR and narrative framing, when the task involves shaping public perception at scale, when historical context on propaganda-to-marketing lineage matters.

### Claude Hopkins
**Role**: Scientific advertising pioneer; father of coupon testing and reason-why copy.
**Contribution**:
Author of Scientific Advertising (1923). Treated advertising as salesmanship in print, measured by coupons and tests. Championed specific, reason-why claims over general boasts.
**When to invoke**: When the work needs direct-response rigor, when writing reason-why copy, when grounding creative decisions in testable propositions.

### Rosser Reeves
**Role**: Chairman of Ted Bates; originator of the Unique Selling Proposition.
**Contribution**:
Chairman of Ted Bates and author of Reality in Advertising (1961). Defined the Unique Selling Proposition: one specific benefit, unique to the brand, strong enough to move people. Pushed repetition of that single claim.
**When to invoke**: When the task requires a single, differentiating claim, when cutting through creative clutter, when the brief demands a USP.

### Leo Burnett
**Role**: Founder of Leo Burnett Company; creator of brand icons and inherent drama.
**Contribution**:
Founder of the Leo Burnett Company, creator of the Marlboro Man, the Jolly Green Giant, and Tony the Tiger. Believed every product has an inherent drama to find. Built brand characters as lasting distinctive assets.
**When to invoke**: When the brand needs a mythic quality, when creating brand characters or icons, when the strategy is to find the inherent drama in the product.

### Raymond Rubicam
**Role**: Co-founder of Young & Rubicam; pioneer of creative-first agency culture.
**Contribution**:
Co-founder of Young & Rubicam. Insisted that advertising must resist the usual ("resist the usual" was the agency's credo) and that creative quality drives results. Early supporter of research (he hired George Gallup) alongside creative ambition.
**When to invoke**: When arguing that creative quality drives business results, when building a creative culture, when the task needs historical grounding in the creative-as-business-advantage tradition.

## Semiotics and Cultural Theory

### Ferdinand de Saussure
**Role**: Linguist; founder of structural linguistics (Course in General Linguistics, 1916).
**Contribution**: Defined the sign as a signifier (form) joined to a signified (concept), linked by convention. Showed that meaning comes from difference within a system, not from things themselves. Basis for reading a brand's colors, words, and forms against the category.
**When to invoke**: When auditing a category's codes, naming, or choosing a color or form that must mean something by contrast.

### Charles Sanders Peirce
**Role**: Philosopher and logician; founder of pragmatism and of semiotics in the US.
**Contribution**: Classified signs as icon (resemblance), index (causal or physical link), and symbol (convention). Gives a precise way to ask how a mark or image signifies. Useful for deciding whether an asset should show, point, or stand for.
**When to invoke**: When designing marks, stamps, icons, and proof assets; when checking whether evidence reads as evidence.

### Roland Barthes
**Role**: Literary theorist and semiologist; author of Mythologies (1957) and "Rhetoric of the Image" (1964).
**Contribution**: Showed how everyday objects and ads carry a second layer of meaning, myth, that makes cultural choices look natural. His reading of the Panzani pasta ad separated literal and connoted messages in an image. Teaches both how to build a myth and how to see one being built.
**When to invoke**: When the work must make a stance feel like common sense; when critiquing a competitor's or category's myth.

### Dick Hebdige
**Role**: Cultural theorist; author of Subculture: The Meaning of Style (1979).
**Contribution**: Read punk, mod, and other subcultures as signifying practices that build meaning through bricolage and refusal. Showed how the mainstream absorbs subcultural style by turning signs into products or explaining them away. The source of Every's recuperation risk monitor.
**When to invoke**: When adopting any sign with edge; when building an in-group style; when judging whether a style is ours or borrowed.

### Pierre Bourdieu
**Role**: Sociologist; author of Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste (1979).
**Contribution**: Showed that cultural preferences classify the people who hold them and track cultural and economic capital. Distinction from the common choice is a social resource. Explains why identifying with a brand can confer status.
**When to invoke**: When the work trades on membership, status, or cultural capital; when deciding how exclusive a signal should feel.

### Susan Sontag
**Role**: Essayist and critic; author of "Notes on 'Camp'" (1964) and Against Interpretation (1966).
**Contribution**: Described camp as a sensibility of artifice, exaggeration, and love of style over content. Showed that a knowing relationship with form can itself be the appeal. Gives Every language for deliberate excess and wit in form.
**When to invoke**: When the work plays with artifice, irony, or excess; when testing whether humor in form is knowing or accidental.

### Glenn O'Brien
**Role**: Writer, editor, and creative director; Interview editor, GQ's Style Guy, author of Like Art: Glenn O'Brien on Advertising (Karma, 2017; collects his Artforum advertising column, 1985–1990), How to Be a Man (2011), and other books.
**Contribution**: Argued that advertising and art have converged until, ideally, the only difference is the logo, and that Pop Art's license to steal ran both ways. Valued ads that transcend their category and use commerce as a vehicle for art; came into advertising writing a Barneys New York spot. Wrote about cool and hipness as a discipline and moved across art, fashion, music, TV (TV Party), and advertising, a model for a creative director who works between culture and commerce. Excerpt read: The Paris Review, Feb. 27, 2017 (theparisreview.org/blog/2017/02/27/like-art/).
**When to invoke**: When work must hold up as an object without its offer; when writing about style; when bridging art and commercial work. Every rejects his "no ethics in advertising" line (see `groundings.md`).

### Marshall McLuhan
**Role**: Media theorist; author of Understanding Media (1964).
**Contribution**: Argued the medium is the message and distinguished hot and cool media by how much participation they ask for. The tetrad asks what a medium enhances, obsolesces, retrieves, and reverses into. See `marketing-science/brand-equity/references/mcluhan-media.md`.
**When to invoke**: When choosing a medium or format; when a new channel changes what the brand can say.

### Guy Debord
**Role**: Situationist writer and filmmaker; author of The Society of the Spectacle (1967).
**Contribution**: Argued that in modern society images stand in for lived relationships between people, and that spectacle absorbs critique. With the Situationists, practiced détournement: turning existing images against their original meaning. A check on empty spectacle.
**When to invoke**: When a moment risks being only an image; when using parody or détournement; when judging whether an event creates real relationships.

### John Berger
**Role**: Art critic, novelist, and painter; author of Ways of Seeing (1972 book and BBC series).
**Contribution**: Showed that seeing comes before words and that ways of seeing are learned. Reproduction detaches images from their settings so meaning depends on context; publicity sells an envied future self; images encode who looks and who is looked at. Grounds Every's art direction in how images actually work on viewers.
**When to invoke**: In any art direction, campaign image, or social visual; when cropping, reposting, or placing an image in a new context.

## Memetics and Contagion

### Richard Dawkins
**Role**: Evolutionary biologist; author of The Selfish Gene (1976).
**Contribution**: Coined "meme" for a unit of cultural transmission that spreads by imitation. Argued replicators succeed through fidelity, fecundity, and longevity. Gives Every a vocabulary for why some ideas copy themselves and others die.
**When to invoke**: When designing formats meant to be copied; when explaining why an idea spread.

### Susan Blackmore
**Role**: Psychologist; author of The Meme Machine (1999).
**Contribution**: Developed memetics into a full theory with imitation as the mechanism. Argued memes compete for limited attention and memory and that much of culture can be read through that competition. Adds rigor about what counts as copying.
**When to invoke**: When competing for attention in a crowded feed; when judging whether a format is being imitated or only seen.

### Limor Shifman
**Role**: Communication scholar; author of "Memes in a Digital World" (2013) and Memes in Digital Culture (2014).
**Contribution**: Defined an internet meme as a group of items sharing content, form, or stance, created with awareness of each other and spread by many users. Distinguished memes, which invite people to make their own version, from single viral items. The most useful definition for social work.
**When to invoke**: When designing participatory social formats and series; when deciding what content, form, or stance Every should own.

### Jonah Berger
**Role**: Marketing professor at Wharton; author of Contagious (2013) and Invisible Influence (2016).
**Contribution**: STEPPS: social currency, triggers, emotion, public, practical value, and stories drive sharing. With Katherine Milkman, found that high-arousal emotion makes content more likely to be shared. Not to be confused with John Berger.
**When to invoke**: When predicting whether a piece will travel; when designing for word of mouth.

### René Girard
**Role**: Literary theorist and anthropologist; author of Deceit, Desire and the Novel (1961).
**Contribution**: Mimetic desire: people want what their models want, so desire is borrowed, not original. Rivalry follows when people want the same things. Explains why a cast of admired practitioners moves desire more than claims do.
**When to invoke**: When choosing who appears in the work; when building aspiration through people rather than product.

### Mike Taylor
**Role**: Every writer; author of Marketing Memetics (2023); co-founder of the agency Ladder.
**Contribution**: Turns memetics into a working method: map a category's memes, copy where the audience expects familiarity and differ where the brand must stand out, and build each idea with bait, hook, reward, and payload. Prioritizes tests by concept, theme, and variant. In-house reference; the Drive copy read so far covers chapters 1–3.2.
**When to invoke**: When mapping a category's formats; when deciding what to copy and where to differ; when planning creative tests.

## Design and Visual Culture

### Bruno Munari
**Role**: Artist and designer; author of Design as Art (1966) and Da cosa nasce cosa (1981).
**Contribution**: Argued the designer is an artist who comes down off the pedestal to work inside everyday life, with no split between beautiful things to look at and ugly things to use. *Da cosa nasce cosa* (1981) set out his method: define the problem, break it into elements, collect and analyze data, then creativity, materials and technologies, experimentation, models, and verification. His useless machines, unreadable books, and children's books show rigor and play together; he is the spine of Every's art direction (`craft/art-direction/references/design-lineage.md`).
**When to invoke**: In all art direction; as the order of operations for new identity or campaign visual work; when a design feels decorative rather than useful.

### Dieter Rams
**Role**: Industrial designer at Braun (1955–1995) and Vitsoe.
**Contribution**: Ten principles: good design is innovative, useful, aesthetic, understandable, unobtrusive, honest, long-lasting, thorough down to the last detail, environmentally friendly, and as little design as possible. "Less, but better." Every applies the principles, not the Braun look.
**When to invoke**: In design review; when cutting elements; when checking honesty and usefulness of form.

### Paul Rand
**Role**: Graphic designer; IBM identity and other corporate marks; author of Thoughts on Design (1947) and "Logos, Flags, and Escutcheons" (AIGA, 1991).
**Contribution**: Held that a logo identifies rather than sells or describes, and that it gets its meaning from the quality of the thing it stands for. Described design as unifying, simplifying, and removing what is superfluous. Sets Every's rules for marks.
**When to invoke**: When making or revising a mark, lockup, stamp, or icon system.

### Saul Bass
**Role**: Graphic designer and filmmaker; title sequences for The Man with the Golden Arm (1955), Vertigo, North by Northwest, and Psycho; marks for AT&T (1969, 1983), Continental (1968), and United (1974).
**Contribution**: Turned the title sequence into designed storytelling and made typography move. Reduced complex stories and companies to single symbols that lasted decades. Saw the work as serving the film it introduced.
**When to invoke**: For video opens, launch films, idents, social motion, event screens, and marks that must animate.

### Milton Glaser
**Role**: Designer and illustrator; co-founded Push Pin Studios (1954) and New York magazine with Clay Felker (1968); made the Bob Dylan poster (1967) and I ♥ NY (1977); author of Art Is Work (2000).
**Contribution**: Brought wit, drawing, and a personal hand into commercial and civic design. Showed that a mark can be a sentence the reader completes, which is why I ♥ NY spread and still points home. Treated editorial design as brand design.
**When to invoke**: When work must be warm, witty, or civic; when a mark should invite participation; for editorial surfaces.

### Ivan Chermayeff
**Role**: Designer and artist; co-founded Chermayeff & Geismar with Tom Geismar in 1957 (now Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv).
**Contribution**: The firm's marks include Chase, Mobil, Pan Am, NBC (modernized 1986), Showtime, National Geographic, the Smithsonian, MoMA, and the Library of Congress, built on bold abstraction, primary color, and strong letterforms. Chermayeff treated modernizing a worn mark as being as important as making a new one, and made found-object collages with dry humor. The model for refreshing and consolidating marks without losing recognition.
**When to invoke**: When refreshing a sub-brand, consolidating competing marks, or making collage-led imagery.

### The Swiss school (International Typographic Style)
**Role**: Ernst Keller (teaching from 1918), Armin Hofmann and Emil Ruder (Basel School of Design, from 1947), Josef Müller-Brockmann (grids; co-editor of Neue Grafik), Max Bill.
**Contribution**: Built design on cleanliness, readability, objectivity, the mathematical grid, asymmetric layout, sans serif type (Akzidenz-Grotesk, Univers, Helvetica), and photography over illustration. Made the grid the standard way to structure information. Every's Founders Grotesk and Mono sit in this lineage.
**When to invoke**: For information-heavy and system surfaces (Skills Library, docs, pricing, data, schedules, components), not for moments that need warmth or a hand.
