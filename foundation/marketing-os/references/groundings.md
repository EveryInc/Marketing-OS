# Groundings

Douglas's philosophical groundings. These lenses come first in any strategy, concept,
campaign, social, naming, or art-direction task, before frameworks and before drafting.
They are how Every's marketing decides what a thing means, whether it will travel, how it
earns a place in the mind, and how a person actually decides. Ruled by Douglas, Nov. 8,
2026, as the foundation for all creative and marketing thinking.

`canon.md` holds the full thinker entries; `reading-log.md` records which were checked
against the text. `method.md` holds Every's positions and the
behavioral economics foundation. This file is the operating layer: the question each
lens asks, the test it applies, and where the lenses pull against each other.

## How to use this file

1. **Opening pass.** Before drafting a strategy, concept, or brief, answer at least one
   question from four different lenses below in plain sentences. Put the answers in the
   working notes, not the deliverable.
2. **Review pass.** Before handing work back, run the tests for every lens the work
   touches. A failed test is a revision, not a footnote.
3. **Name the lens in the argument when it carries the argument.** "This works because
   the folder is a sign people already use for their own competence" is grounded. "This
   is semiotically rich" is decoration.
4. **Keep references doing work.** A thinker cited without a consequence for the work is
   name-dropping. Cut it.

## 1. Signs and myth: semiotics

**Core texts.** Ferdinand de Saussure, *Course in General Linguistics* (1916): the sign
as signifier plus signified, and meaning as difference within a system. Charles Sanders
Peirce: icon, index, and symbol. Roland Barthes, *Mythologies* (1957): myth as a second
layer of meaning that makes a cultural or commercial choice look natural.

**What it teaches.** Nothing in a brand is neutral. Every color, word, crop, and format
signifies through its difference from the rest of the category. Barthes adds the
commercial point: the strongest marketing turns a choice into common sense, and the
critic's job is to see the myth being made.

**Questions.** What does this signify beyond what it literally says? Which code is it
using: Every's, or the category's default? What myth does it make look natural, and do we
want to own that myth?

**Tests.**
- Name the signifier, the first-order meaning, and the second-order myth for the work's
  main sign.
- Run the competitive semiotic audit (`craft/art-direction/references/competitive-semiotic-audit.md`)
  when the work enters a crowded visual or verbal code.
- Swap the logo (Neumeier's swap test). If the sign now reads as a competitor's, the code
  is the category's.

## 2. How images make meaning: ways of seeing

**Core texts.** John Berger, *Ways of Seeing* (1972 book and BBC series).

**What it teaches.** Seeing comes before words, and how we see is learned. Reproduction
detaches an image from its original setting, so its meaning depends on where and next to
what it appears. Publicity sells the viewer an envied version of their future self, and
images carry a point of view about who looks and who is looked at.

**Questions.** Who is looking, from where, and at whom? What future self is this image
selling? What does the image mean once it is cropped, screenshotted, and reposted next to
something else?

**Tests.**
- State the viewer's position and the self the work invites them to become.
- Check that the envied self is one Every can deliver, not glamour borrowed from a
  lifestyle the product does not touch.
- Look at the asset in its real context: a feed, a phone, a stage screen, a newsletter.

## 3. Design as art: Munari, Rams, and the design lineage

**Core texts.** Bruno Munari, *Design as Art* (1966) and *Da cosa nasce cosa* (1981), the
book that sets out his design method. Dieter Rams's ten principles of good design and
"less, but better." The lineage art direction is measured against: Paul Rand, Saul Bass,
Milton Glaser, Ivan Chermayeff with Chermayeff & Geismar, and the Swiss school where the
surface calls for it. The working rules live in
`craft/art-direction/references/design-lineage.md`.

**What it teaches.**
- Munari: the designer is an artist working inside everyday life, not above it. Good
  design is made for use and for understanding, it lets the object explain itself, and
  simplifying is harder than complicating. His method puts creativity fifth, after the
  problem, its elements, data collection, and analysis.
- Rams: good design is innovative, useful, aesthetic, understandable, unobtrusive, honest,
  long-lasting, thorough down to the last detail, environmentally friendly, and as little
  design as possible.
- Rand: a mark identifies; it gets its meaning from the quality of what it stands for.
- Bass: reduce the story to one symbol, and let the symbol move.
- Glaser: wit and a personal hand belong in commercial work; a mark can be a sentence the
  reader completes.
- Chermayeff & Geismar: bold abstraction, and modernizing a worn mark without losing what
  people recognize.
- The Swiss school: grid, objectivity, and readable sans serif type for information-heavy
  surfaces.

**Questions.** What is the problem, and what are its elements, before any visual? Does the
form explain what the thing is and how to use it? What can be removed without losing
meaning? Is any part decoration standing in for an idea?

**Tests.**
- State the problem and its elements before showing a territory (Munari's order).
- Remove one element at a time. If meaning survives, the element goes.
- A first-time viewer can say what the thing is and what to do next within a few seconds.
- No false promise in the form: the design does not make the product look more capable,
  finished, or valuable than it is.
- Borrow the reasoning of the lineage, never the look. A quoted Bass poster or Swiss grid
  without its reasoning is pastiche.

## 4. Style as meaning: subculture, distinction, camp, and advertising as art

**Core texts.** Dick Hebdige, *Subculture: The Meaning of Style* (1979). Pierre Bourdieu,
*Distinction* (1979). Susan Sontag, "Notes on 'Camp'" (1964). Glenn O'Brien's writing,
especially *Like Art: Glenn O'Brien on Advertising* (Karma, 2017), which collects his
*Artforum* advertising column, which ran from 1985 to 1990 (dates per the book's
excerpt in *The Paris Review*, Feb. 27, 2017), plus his work at *Interview*, as GQ's
Style Guy, and in advertising.

**What it teaches.**
- Hebdige: subcultures build meaning by taking everyday objects and recombining them
  (bricolage). Style is a signifying practice. The mainstream then absorbs it, either by
  turning the signs into products or by explaining the group away. That absorption is the
  constant risk to anything with edge.
- Bourdieu: preferences classify the people who hold them. Cultural capital and
  distinction from the common choice are what make a brand worth identifying with.
- Sontag: camp is a sensibility of artifice and exaggeration, a way of seeing style over
  content. It shows that a knowing relationship with form can itself be the appeal.
- O'Brien: advertising and art have converged until, ideally, the only difference is the
  logo. Pop Art took from advertising, and advertising took the license back. The ads
  worth making transcend their category and use commerce to carry art. Cool is a
  discipline, hipness runs on knowledge passed inside a group, and style is wit put to
  practical use.

**Questions.** Who would recognize this as theirs, and who would not get it? What
distinction does it confer on the person who shares it? Is this a sign we made, or one we
borrowed from a group that will see it as taken? Would it hold up as an object on its own,
with the logo covered?

**Tests.**
- Name the in-group that reads it fully and what it signals about them.
- Check for borrowed style: if the sign belongs to a subculture Every is not part of, cut
  it or credit the source.
- Run the recuperation risk monitor in `marketing-science/brand-equity` for any sign
  Every depends on.
- The "like art" test: would someone keep, frame, or screenshot it with no offer attached?

## 5. Ideas that copy themselves: memetics and contagion

**Core texts.** Richard Dawkins, *The Selfish Gene* (1976), which coined "meme." Susan
Blackmore, *The Meme Machine* (1999). Limor Shifman, "Memes in a Digital World:
Reconciling with a Conceptual Troublemaker" (2013) and *Memes in Digital Culture* (2014).
Jonah Berger, *Contagious* (2013), and Berger and Milkman on emotion and sharing. René
Girard on mimetic desire, from *Deceit, Desire and the Novel* (1961). Seth Godin,
*Unleashing the Ideavirus* (2000). In-house: Mike
Taylor, *Marketing Memetics* (2023).

**What it teaches.**
- Dawkins: ideas behave like replicators. The ones that spread copy accurately, copy
  often, and last.
- Blackmore: imitation is the mechanism, and memes compete for limited attention and
  memory.
- Shifman: an internet meme is a group of items sharing content, form, or stance, made
  with awareness of each other. Memes invite people to make their own version, which is
  different from a single viral item.
- Jonah Berger (STEPPS): people share what gives them social currency, what everyday
  triggers bring to mind, what stirs high-arousal emotion, what is visible to others,
  what is practically useful, and what comes wrapped in a story.
- Girard: people want what the people they admire want. Desire is borrowed from a model.
- Godin: word of mouth dies out unless something amplifies it. Seed the idea with
  sneezers inside a hive, make it smooth enough to get at a glance, and plan the amplifier.
- Taylor: map what already works in a category, copy where the audience needs
  familiarity, differ where the brand must be recognized, and build each meme with bait,
  hook, reward, and payload.

**Questions.** What is the template someone else could copy? Which of content, form, and
stance is ours? Who is the model whose desire this borrows? What survives when someone
remixes it, and does Every's name survive with it?

**Tests.**
- Write the template in one line ("show us your folders": a person opens their working
  files and explains their system).
- Identify the model the audience admires and what they are shown wanting.
- Check STEPPS: at least two of the six are strong.
- Check the payload: after three remixes, the idea still points back to Every.
- Name the sneezers, the hive, and the amplifier (Godin).

## 6. Owning a place in the mind: positioning and differentiation

**Core texts.** Al Ries and Jack Trout, *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981). David
Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983). Read against Byron Sharp and Jenni Romaniuk on
distinctiveness and mental availability.

**What it teaches.**
- Ries and Trout: positioning happens in the prospect's mind, not in the product. Own a
  word, be first in a category or make a new one, and position against the leader when
  you can't be first.
- Ogilvy: research before creative, a big idea people remember, a consistent brand image
  over years, and respect for the reader's intelligence.
- Sharp and Romaniuk: memory and recognition matter more than reasons to choose, so
  distinctive assets and broad reach carry weight.

**Brand equity models behind this lens.** Positioning sets the word; the equity models say
what a brand must build around it. David Aaker's identity system, Kevin Lane Keller's
brand resonance pyramid, Jean-Noël Kapferer's identity prism, and Douglas Holt's cultural
branding (icons resolve tensions their audience lives with) each have a full reference in
`marketing-science/brand-equity/references/` (`aaker.md`, `keller.md`, `kapferer.md`,
`holt.md`). Load the relevant one whenever the work defines or changes what the brand
stands for, not only for formal equity evaluations.

**How Every reconciles them.** Differentiate in meaning (the word and the myth Every owns)
and be distinctive in memory (the assets that make it Every's on sight). One without the
other fails: a sharp position nobody can recognize, or a recognizable look that stands
for nothing.

**Questions.** What word does Every own in this audience's mind? Who is the leader we're
positioned against? Which distinctive asset ties this work to Every without the logo?

**Tests.**
- State the owned word or idea in under ten words.
- Neumeier's hand test: cover the logo. If you can't tell who's talking, the voice isn't
  distinctive. His field test: if the audience can't say the concept back, it wasn't
  communicated.
- Name the competitor or default the work positions against.
- Name the distinctive asset carried and check it is used consistently.

## 7. How decisions actually get made: behavioral economics

**Core texts.** Daniel Kahneman, Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein, Dan Ariely, Robert
Cialdini, Rory Sutherland. The full foundation and ethics line live in `method.md`
("Behavioral integration").

**Questions.** What frame does the reader meet first? What's the default? Where's the
friction, and is it in the right place? Is the value psychological, material, or both?

**Tests.**
- Name the frame, the default, and the friction in the path.
- Remove sludge from the action we want; never add friction to leaving.
- No dark patterns, per `method.md`.

## 8. Images and real relationships: the spectacle

**Core text.** Guy Debord, *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967), with the Situationist
practice of détournement (turning an existing image or message against its original use).

**What it teaches.** "The spectacle is not a collection of images; it is a social relation
between people that is mediated by images" (thesis 4). Under it, "dissatisfaction itself
becomes a commodity" (thesis 59): critique is absorbed and sold back. Détournement is "the
opposite of quotation" (thesis 208).

**Questions.** Does this moment create a relationship between people, or only an image of
one? If it turns someone else's image against itself, is the turn sharp enough not to be
absorbed back into the thing it mocks?

**Tests.**
- Count the people who meet, talk, build, or reply because of the work, not only the
  people who view it.
- For any détournement, check that the original's meaning flips, not merely repeats.

## Where the lenses pull against each other

- **Memetics wants copying; distinction wants scarcity.** Make the template easy to copy
  and the source hard to fake. The form can spread; the authorship stays Every's.
- **Ries and Trout want difference; Sharp wants distinctiveness.** Use both: meaning for
  difference, assets for memory (section 6).
- **O'Brien says advertising and art converge, and that there are no ethics in
  advertising.** Every takes the first half and rejects the second. Trust is the asset;
  `method.md`'s ethics line holds.
- **Munari and Rams want less; camp wants more.** Restraint is the default. Excess is a
  deliberate, signed choice for a specific moment, never a habit.
- **The Swiss grid wants objectivity; Glaser wants a hand.** Grid for information, hand for
  feeling. The two can share a surface.
- **The spectacle absorbs critique.** Any edgy sign Every adopts needs a plan for when it
  is copied by the category.
