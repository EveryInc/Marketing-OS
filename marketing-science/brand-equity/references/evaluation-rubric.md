# Evaluation Rubric — Brand Equity Scoring at Every

Most brand equity frameworks produce a single number or a verdict. This rubric produces a shape — a profile across three dimensions that maps where a brand stands against where its strategy requires it to be. The shape is the thing. An absolute score without a target is noise.

Three dimensions are scored independently: Identity Clarity, Cognitive Presence, and Cultural Position. Each has its own failure mode, its own theoretical lineage, and its own scoring sub-components. Together they cover the supply side (what the brand is constructing and projecting), the demand side (what the audience actually holds in memory and how it relates to the brand), and the cultural side (what role the brand plays in the social economy of meaning). A brand can be strong on two and failing on one in ways that produce catastrophically wrong strategic diagnoses if you're only looking at the aggregate.

---

## On Byron Sharp

Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010) synthesizes Andrew Ehrenberg's empirical research on buying behavior in fast-moving consumer goods. The core findings are real: heavy buyers are rare, penetration matters more than loyalty farming, and most category growth comes from reaching light and non-buyers rather than deepening engagement with existing ones. These findings hold in categories with high purchase frequency, low-involvement decisions, and functionally interchangeable competitors. In CPG, they are well-evidenced.

What Sharp contributes to the ideas of "mental availability" and "distinctive brand assets" is modest: he popularized them. Mental availability traces to Tversky and Kahneman's availability heuristic (1973) — the probability a stimulus is retrieved in context is a function of encoding strength, retrieval cues, and recency, not exposure frequency as such. Distinctive brand assets trace to semiotic theory — Peirce's trichotomy of icon, index, and symbol; Barthes on connotation and mythological function; de Saussure on arbitrary and motivated signs — traditions Sharp does not engage. Both concepts are more precisely understood through their original sources, which is where this rubric routes.

The structural limitation is scope. Sharp's empirical generalizations apply to functional purchase categories. They break in low-frequency, high-involvement, identity-expressive categories, precisely because these categories violate his modeling assumptions. Badge brands contradict double-jeopardy predictions: their light buyers are disproportionately loyal and their heavy buyers are identity-committed. Loyalty exists; it is structurally different in categories where the purchase is partly about who the buyer signals they are. Differentiation exists; it operates on a cultural axis rather than a functional one. Every and its sub-brands operate entirely in this territory. We note Sharp here because he is the dominant voice in the marketing-effectiveness conversation and because practitioners trained on his work will encounter his concepts throughout the OS. He is not a load-bearing reference.

## Three Dimensions

**Identity Clarity** — Does the brand know what it is, say it consistently, and mean it? This is the supply side: the structured meanings the brand is actively constructing and projecting into the world.

**Cognitive Presence** — Does the reference audience hold the brand in memory in the right way, with the right associations, and does it produce loyalty and resonance among that audience? This is the demand side: what the audience actually carries.

**Cultural Position** — Does the brand occupy a real position in the social economy of meaning? Does it address a live cultural tension, generate the right kind of cultural capital, function as a usable status object, and resist recuperation? This is the cultural side: what role the brand plays in the world beyond the exchange.

All three are scored 1–5 at the sub-component level. Dimension scores are unweighted averages of their sub-components. The rubric produces absolute scores and a target-shape overlay. How to set targets is addressed in the scoring architecture section.

## Dimension 1: Identity Clarity

Identity Clarity measures whether a brand has a stable, coherent, ownable structure of meaning. Anchored in Aaker's brand identity system and supplemented by two of Kapferer's prism facets, this dimension scores what the brand is sending, not what the audience is receiving.

Aaker's architecture establishes three nested structures. Core identity is the small set of associations that are timeless and central — the two to four things that should survive a product pivot, a market shift, or a leadership change. Extended identity is the surrounding texture: personality, sub-brand relationships, visual and verbal habits, the things that give the core fullness without constituting it. Brand essence is the single organizing thought that would, if you held it in mind, make every correct brand decision derivable from it. It is not a tagline — it is the logic behind the taglines. The four Aaker perspectives (brand-as-product, brand-as-organization, brand-as-person, brand-as-symbol) provide a systematic way to check whether the identity is fully inhabited across the registers a brand occupies, or whether some registers are vacant.

Kapferer's identity prism supplements Aaker with two facets Aaker underweights. The culture facet is the ideological value system the brand embodies — not its personality, but its cosmology: what does this brand believe about how to live or work well? This is the level at which brand identity becomes a worldview rather than a persona. The self-image facet is the internal mirror: what does a person believe about themselves when they use this brand? For badge brands this is the primary mechanism of value. A brand with a fully articulated Aaker identity can still fail the self-image test — if using the brand tells the user nothing specific about who they are, it cannot function as a badge. Both facets are sender-side: they represent what the brand intends to transmit.

Distinctive brand assets are scored here rather than under Cognitive Presence. The semiotic tradition explains why: an asset earns its power not only through repeated exposure and memory encoding but through the type of sign relationship it establishes. Peirce's trichotomy is the relevant taxonomy. An icon resembles its referent (a realistic photograph of a product). An index is causally or physically connected to what it signifies (smoke signifies fire; a brand mark worn by a certain kind of person becomes connected to that kind of person by co-presence). A symbol is arbitrary and conventional — the connection is established only through repeated association. Assets that operate as indices are categorically more powerful than assets that function only as arbitrary symbols, because indexical connection implies the presence of a whole world, not just the recognition of a name. Barthes' connotation layer captures why brand assets accumulate meaning over time: repeated cultural use adds a mythological function to the denotative one — the asset begins to mean a way of life, not just a company. This sub-component rewards depth of semiotic investment, not just asset recognition or recall rate.

### Sub-components and Scoring

Scale: 1–5, integers preferred. Half-points permitted when evidence clearly warrants; do not use half-points as hedges.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 5 | Fully realized — the standard is high and consistently met |
| 4 | Strong — well-executed with minor gaps or inconsistencies |
| 3 | Functional — present and legible, lacking depth or distinction |
| 2 | Nascent — thin, contradictory, or dependent on a single touchpoint |
| 1 | Absent or incoherent |

**1.1 Core identity** — Is there a stable, small set of associations that are timeless, central, and would survive a pivot? Can you state what the brand is without listing features or describing the current product?

**1.2 Extended identity coherence** — Do the peripheral elements (tone, visual system, personality, partnerships, sub-brand relationships) cohere around and enrich the core? Or do they pull in contradictory directions?

**1.3 Brand essence** — Is there a single organizing thought from which every correct brand decision is derivable? Distinct from a tagline — this is the idea behind the taglines. Score both whether it exists and whether it is actually generative (applied to a new creative problem, does it produce a clear direction?).

**1.4 Four perspectives coverage** — Scored as a set. Does the brand operate coherently as product (what it does), organization (what it stands for institutionally), person (what personality it has), and symbol (what assets carry its meaning)? Note which perspective, if any, is underinhabited.

**1.5 Kapferer: culture facet** — What ideological value system does the brand embody at the level of cosmology — what world does it come from, what does it believe? This is not the brand's personality or tone. A brand with a fully developed culture facet operates from a position: it has something at stake in how the world turns out.

**1.6 Kapferer: self-image facet** — Who does the user believe they are when they use this brand? Score both: (a) whether the intended self-image is clearly articulated by the brand, and (b) whether it is coherent with the core identity. A self-image that contradicts the core identity creates equity that belongs to the user's projection, not the brand.

**1.7 Distinctive asset depth** — Are the brand's non-name assets (verbal, visual, sonic, behavioral) semiotically rich enough to function as indices or connotation-carriers rather than mere recognition cues? An asset that triggers brand recognition without triggering brand meaning — that identifies without evoking — is weak regardless of its recall rate.

**Dimension 1 score** = unweighted average of 1.1–1.7. Any sub-component below 3 is a priority gap. Note which Aaker perspective is weakest.

## Dimension 2: Cognitive Presence

Cognitive Presence measures the depth and quality of the brand's representation in the minds of its reference audience — what the audience holds in memory, how strongly, and what behavioral and emotional relationships follow from it. Anchored in Keller's Customer-Based Brand Equity pyramid and the behavioral science of memory and retrieval, this dimension scores the demand side.

The reference audience is not the total addressable market. This is the critical methodological decision before any scoring begins. A24 measured against all moviegoers shows weak salience; measured against film-serious audiences it shows strong salience and high resonance. Every master measured against all knowledge workers shows weak presence; measured against early-adopter AI-adjacent professionals it shows something diagnostically useful. Audience definition determines what scores mean. Set it wrong and the dimension scores tell you nothing.

Keller's pyramid is a diagnostic as much as it is a developmental sequence. The stages — salience, then performance and imagery associations, then judgments and feelings, then resonance — describe both the order in which equity builds and the pattern in which it degrades. A brand with high resonance among a small committed audience and low salience more broadly is in a healthy early-stage position for a badge brand; the rubric should capture this as strength in a narrow band rather than aggregate weakness. Stage mismatches are diagnostically important: high imagery associations combined with low performance beliefs indicates an aspiration-credibility gap; high judgments combined with low resonance indicates a quality perception that is not converting into relationship.

Category entry points are scored for Studio products, where clear purchase occasions exist — I need a writing tool, an email management tool, a research tool. For Editorial products — Every master as newsletter bundle — CEPs are less structurally applicable. The relevant construct for Editorial is identity activation: not which memory is retrieved when the consumer faces a functional need, but which brand the consumer reaches for when they are constructing a story about who they are and what they read. This operates differently from purchase occasion and should be scored differently.

Tversky and Kahneman's availability heuristic (1973, "Availability: A Heuristic for Judging Frequency and Probability") provides the correct grounding for salience scoring. Availability is ease of retrieval in a relevant context — determined by encoding strength, cue specificity, and recency of activation, not by exposure frequency. High salience within the reference audience means the brand is among the first things retrieved when a relevant need or identity moment arises, not that it is widely recognized in an undifferentiated population.

### Defining the Reference Audience

Required before scoring this dimension. State explicitly:

- Who the target audience is (role, disposition, relationship to the category and to Every)
- Why this audience definition rather than a broader or narrower one
- What evidence exists that the brand currently has presence with this specific audience

The reference audience should match the brand's current targeting strategy, not its aspirational long-term audience — unless you are performing a gap analysis against the growth target, in which case label it as such.

### Sub-components and Scoring

Same 1–5 scale as Dimension 1.

**2.1 Salience within reference audience** — In contexts where the brand is relevant, does it come to mind readily and early? High salience means the brand is among the first retrieved when the reference audience faces a relevant need, identity moment, or purchase decision.

**2.2 Performance associations** — What does the reference audience believe the brand does, functionally? Are those beliefs accurate to what the brand actually delivers? Are they differentiated from category alternatives in ways that matter to the audience?

**2.3 Imagery associations** — Who does the reference audience believe uses this brand, and what does that say about the user? For badge brands, imagery associations are co-equal with performance associations: the meaning you receive from being associated with the brand is part of what the brand delivers.

**2.4 Judgments: quality and credibility** — What is the reference audience's overall assessment of the brand's quality and trustworthiness? Strong performance associations can coexist with weak credibility if the brand has not converted beliefs into trust through consistent delivery.

**2.5 Feelings** — What emotional responses does the brand consistently elicit? Score both valence (positive/negative) and specificity. Generic positive emotion (inspired, excited) scores at best a 3. A specific, distinctive emotional signature — the particular feeling that distinguishes this brand from every other thing the audience finds good — scores a 4 or 5.

**2.6 Resonance** — Keller's four resonance dimensions: behavioral loyalty (repurchase, retention), attitudinal attachment (advocacy, recommendation), sense of community (does the audience feel they share something with other brand users?), active engagement (unprompted participation — sharing, creating, identifying publicly with the brand). High resonance in a small audience is the asset you build from; do not discount it because the audience is small.

**2.7 CEP coverage or identity activation** — For Studio products: how many relevant category entry points does the brand own in the reference audience's memory, and how strongly? For Editorial products: how strongly is the brand associated with a specific subscriber identity cue — what does reading this tell people about who I am?

**Dimension 2 score** = unweighted average of 2.1–2.7. Note any stage-level mismatch (strong at one Keller layer, anomalously weak at the next) as a diagnostic observation distinct from the gap analysis.

## Dimension 3: Cultural Position

Cultural Position measures the brand's depth of roots in culture — whether it addresses a real and live cultural tension, whether it generates and carries the right kind of cultural capital, whether its sign system is coherent and resistant to commodification, whether it functions as an active prop in identity performance, and whether the meaning it intends to transmit is actually transferring. This dimension draws from Holt, Bourdieu, Hebdige, Agamben, McCracken, Goffman, and W. David Marx.

The theoretical lineage matters because each thinker captures a distinct failure mode. Holt (*How Brands Become Icons*, 2004; *Cultural Strategy*, 2010) without Bourdieu mistakes narrative for positioning: a brand can have a compelling cultural myth that no one uses as a status signal because the myth does not map onto a recognized field of distinction. Bourdieu without Hebdige misses the constitutive threat to badge brands: recuperation is how authentic cultural positions become fashion, then cliché, then nothing. Hebdige (*Subculture: The Meaning of Style*, 1979) without Agamben misses the temporal dimension: a sign system assembled with genuine cultural intelligence in 2018 may be dead language by 2026 not because the brand declined but because the taste vocabulary aged out of the moment. Goffman (*The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, 1959) and McCracken (*Culture and Consumption*, 1988) supply the mechanism: they explain how cultural meaning actually transfers from the brand to the person using it, which is the process that makes the rest of this matter. W. David Marx (*Status and Culture*, 2022) provides the contemporary synthesis: status incentives drive cultural change, and brands that function as status objects do so by allowing the bearer to claim a position within a recognized hierarchy of taste or knowledge.

### Sub-components and Scoring

Same 1–5 scale.

**3.1 Cultural tension** — What unresolved contradiction in the current culture does this brand address? Score both: (a) whether the tension is real and live in the present moment (not a tension that resolved five years ago), and (b) whether the brand has articulated it with precision. A vague tension ("people want authenticity"; "the pace of change is overwhelming") scores a 2. A structurally specific tension that names both sides of the contradiction and implies a particular audience's position within it scores a 4–5.

**3.2 Myth resolution** — How effectively does the brand's output — its editorial, products, aesthetic, partnerships, events — resolve the tension it named? Resolution does not mean eliminating the tension. It means giving the audience a stable position in relation to it: a way of being the person who has worked out where they stand. Score both quality of the myth and consistency of its execution.

**3.3 Cultural capital type and field** — What type of cultural capital does association with this brand confer? In which Bourdieusian field does it operate — intellectual, artistic, educational, economic? Is the field right for the brand's strategic audience? Cultural capital invested in the wrong field for the target audience is currency that cannot be spent. Also score whether the brand controls any of the arbitration mechanisms in its field (does it have the credibility to say what counts?).

**3.4 Distinction legibility** — Is the distinction signal legible to exactly the right people, and not broadly legible to everyone? A signal that anyone can read is not a distinction signal — it is style without status function. The correct score here is not "how many people understand this" but "does the right audience understand this, and does understanding it confirm membership?"

**3.5 Bricolage coherence** — How coherently assembled is the brand's sign system from its cultural sources? Bricolage in Hebdige's sense is the recombination of existing cultural materials into new sign configurations — reassigning objects and references to new contexts in ways that generate meaning. Coherent bricolage reads as intentional, generative, and specific to a world: the references connect to a logic. Incoherent bricolage reads as arbitrary cultural borrowing — a mood board without a point of view.

**3.6 Recuperation risk** — How exposed is the brand to having its authentic cultural position absorbed by the mainstream, neutralized, and returned to it as empty style? Recuperation is the mechanism by which subcultural meaning is defused: the style is adopted without the stance, the aesthetic is reproduced without the values, and what was a distinction signal becomes a fashion signal and then a commodity signal. Score whether the brand has structural defenses — difficulty of entry, continuous original cultural production, community as a moat, required knowledge or sensibility — or whether it is primarily reliant on surface style.

Canary signals — observable evidence that recuperation is underway. Monitor at regular cadence; check at every evaluation. These are external events, not feelings:

- Brand appears in mainstream press category roundups (Wired, The Verge, CNET, major tech newsletters with broad undifferentiated audiences) without distinguishing framing — reviewed as an option rather than cited as a taste signal
- Brand appears in YouTube or social comparison content positioned against generic category competitors on feature lists, price, or ease-of-use, with no reference to who uses it or what that says about the user
- The brand's distinctive verbal assets — specific framings, vocabulary, or conceptual moves that originated as part of its cultural position — begin appearing in competitor marketing or in undifferentiated category discourse, stripped of their original context
- Brand is cited positively by audiences whose self-described identity is explicitly outside the original target (for Every Agent: productivity-hustle and "tools I use" accounts; for Every master: mainstream AI news aggregators and generic newsletter roundups; for Cora: email-productivity content with no taste dimension)
- Imagery associations among the original target audience begin collapsing toward performance associations only: users describe the brand in purely functional terms without reference to the social meaning of using it
- The brand's visual or aesthetic language begins appearing in competitor products, generic SaaS design trend reports, or category templates, decoupled from the intellectual context that generated it

When two or more canary signals appear within the same evaluation window, schedule an off-cycle Cultural Position evaluation. Note this score separately as an ongoing monitoring obligation regardless of the overall dimension score.

**3.7 Taste historicity** — Is the brand's taste vocabulary alive in the current cultural moment? Taste is a historically emergent faculty (Agamben) — it is not a fixed quality but a set of capacities that develop and shift as the conditions of cultural production change. A brand whose taste vocabulary is indexed to a previous moment — deploying references, aesthetics, and cultural stances that were live in 2017 but are no longer generating meaning in 2026 — is speaking a language that still sounds like taste to the brand but may sound dated or nostalgic to its target audience. Score not "is this brand tasteful" but "is this brand's taste vocabulary currently alive?"

**3.8 Badge function** — How effectively does this brand serve as a prop in identity performance? Goffman: social actors use props to establish and confirm role and status in interaction. Marx: status incentives drive cultural change, and brands become status objects when they allow bearers to claim a recognized position in a taste or knowledge hierarchy. Score: does the target audience actively use this brand — by subscribing visibly, referencing it in conversation, recommending it, choosing to be identified with it — to signal something specific about who they are?

**3.9 Meaning transfer** — Are the cultural meanings the brand intends to absorb from culture actually reaching the brand, and from the brand reaching the user? McCracken's model: meaning resides first in the culturally constituted world, transfers to brands through editorial content, collaborations, advertising, and cultural positioning, then transfers from brands to individuals through consumption rituals and social use. A breakdown at the first transfer (intended cultural meaning is not landing on the brand) is an output and editorial problem. A breakdown at the second transfer (meaning is on the brand but not reaching users) is a ritual, community, or social proof problem. Both types of failure produce a brand that feels like it has cultural position from the inside and reads as generic from the outside.

**Dimension 3 score** = unweighted average of 3.1–3.9. Flag recuperation risk (3.6) separately as an ongoing monitor regardless of total score.

## Scoring Architecture

### Absolute scores

Dimension scores are unweighted averages of their sub-components, rounded to one decimal place. Do not stop at the dimension score. The sub-component profile is where the strategic information lives. A Dimension 1 score of 3.4 masking a 5 on core identity and a 1 on brand essence is a different problem from a 3.4 distributed evenly across sub-components — one requires a focused intervention on brand essence; the other requires a general lift across the whole dimension.

Report both the dimension score and the sub-component profile. Note any sub-component below 3 as a priority gap.

### Target-shape overlay

The target shape establishes where a brand's strategy requires it to be on each dimension. Not every brand should maximize every dimension — a brand that does this looks like every other ambitious brand, which is the precondition for commodity status. The target shape is specific to each brand's strategic posture.

Express targets as target scores per dimension (1–5). The overlay compares absolute score to target score:

- **Overinvested**: absolute score materially above target (more than 0.5 above). Not necessarily problematic — it depends on what you're overinvested in relative to what you're underinvested in. A brand that is above-target on Cultural Position and below-target on Cognitive Presence has cultural capital that isn't converting into presence. That is a resource allocation opportunity, not a brand problem.
- **On-target**: absolute score within 0.5 of target. The brand is where its strategy requires it to be.
- **Underinvested**: absolute score materially below target (more than 0.5 below). Priority gap.

Because targets differ per brand, do not compare absolute dimension scores across brands without first comparing each against its own target. Every Agent at 3.2 on Cognitive Presence may be on-target; Cora at 3.2 on Cognitive Presence may be significantly underinvested. The absolute scores are comparable only within the same brand over time.

### Setting target shapes per brand

Target shapes are derived from three questions:

**1. What is the primary mechanism by which this brand creates and retains value?** A brand whose value is primarily functional needs high Cognitive Presence in the relevant purchase occasion. A brand whose value is primarily social — where the exchange is identity-expressive — needs high Cultural Position. A brand whose value is primarily epistemic — where the audience is buying a worldview and a place to stand in relation to a set of ideas — needs high Identity Clarity as the foundation. Most brands combine mechanisms; the question is what the primary weight is.

**2. What is the reference audience, and what does this category mean to them?** A narrow, taste-committed audience amplifies Cultural Position signals — small is fine as long as the right people are paying attention. A broad professional audience with a functional job-to-be-done requires high Cognitive Presence in a well-defined purchase occasion. The audience's relationship to the category determines which dimension delivers the most leverage.

**3. What stage of brand development is this brand in?** Early-stage brands should target Identity Clarity first — a brand that does not know what it is cannot build the other dimensions coherently. Cultural Position is the hardest dimension to build and the slowest to decay. Cognitive Presence in a narrow audience is the fastest to build through consistent output. The sequence matters: Identity Clarity is the foundation, Cultural Position and Cognitive Presence build from it.

**4. Does this Studio product serve a proof-point function for Every master?** Studio products that are prominent enough to be read as evidence of Every's broader thesis — that editorial-led companies build software with genuine craft and point of view — should carry higher Cultural Position targets than products serving primarily utility functions in relative obscurity. The brand equity of a visible Studio product compounds into Every master's equity; modest Cultural Position at the product level is a drag on the parent brand's cultural credibility. Apply this factor to Every Agent and Cora now. Keep Monologue's target unset until its positioning and proof-point role are developed.

## Supplementary Lens: McLuhan

After completing the three-dimension scoring, run McLuhan's media tetrad as a second pass. This is not a fourth scored dimension — it produces qualitative observations, not numbers. Its purpose is to surface what the brand is doing to its cultural environment, not merely how it is positioned within it.

McLuhan's argument: every medium (technology, institution, brand) has four effects on its cultural environment, operating simultaneously. The tetrad asks:

- **Enhances** — What does this brand amplify or intensify? What capacity, experience, or aspiration does it extend?
- **Obsolesces** — What does it push aside or make feel inadequate? What does the brand make unnecessary, dated, or vaguely embarrassing to be associated with?
- **Retrieves** — What older cultural form or value does it bring back from obsolescence? Most genuinely iconic brands retrieve something: A24 retrieves auteur cinema from the blockbuster era; The New Yorker retrieves the idea that reading is a form of civic seriousness. The retrieval is part of the value proposition.
- **Reverses** — If extended to its extreme, what does this brand flip into? McLuhan's reversal is a warning about overextension — the thing the brand becomes when it succeeds too completely or grows past the conditions that made it meaningful.

The hot/cool diagnostic applies here. McLuhan's hot medium is high-definition, fills in information for the receiver, requires low participation. Cool medium is low-definition, leaves gaps the receiver must complete, requires high participation. Every is structurally cool — the essays require active interpretation, the software requires the user to bring intelligence and judgment. Heating up a cool medium — making it more accessible, more explicit, more self-explanatory — destroys the quality that makes it valuable. This applies directly to any decision about Every's editorial or product direction that trades specificity and difficulty for reach. The way to grow a cool medium is not to make it hotter; it is to expand the audience capable of participating in it at the required level.

**Temperature-medium fit check.** A brand's communication temperature should match the temperature of its primary medium. A mismatch is a diagnostic finding worth naming.

Mapping Every's primary media by temperature: long-form essays and editorial are cool — text invites completion; the reader supplies context, interpretation, and the connections the author leaves open. Software is cooler than most people assume — users co-author meaning through use; a well-designed interface is a set of possibilities, not a finished object. Podcasts are warmer than newsletters but cooler than video. Short-form video is hot. Social posts vary: a thread that reads as a fragment of a longer argument is cooler than a packaged take that closes its own meaning.

The most common failure mode for content companies trying to scale is building a hot brand on cool media. It looks like overproduction (every format, high volume), overstatement (every piece a declaration), and overdetermination (every piece concluding its own meaning for the reader, leaving nothing to complete). The audience disengages because the participation requirement has been eliminated — which is precisely why the audience came. The second failure mode — building a cold brand on hot media — is rarer: investing in high-definition, fully-packaged formats while claiming the depth that requires audience work. Neither mismatch is automatically fatal, but both are inefficiencies that compound over time.

Running the check: identify the brand's primary medium and assess its temperature. Then assess whether the brand's communication style — its definitional confidence, production density, relationship to ambiguity, and degree to which it closes or opens meaning — matches. Flag mismatches as findings; note whether they are intentional strategic choices or defaults to be corrected.

Record McLuhan observations in the evaluation document after the scored sections, in a section labeled "Medium Analysis."

## Portfolio View

The meaningful comparison across the portfolio is not absolute score vs. absolute score but strategic execution: how close is each brand to its target shape? A brand at (3.5, 3.0, 4.0) that is on-target across all three dimensions is more strategically coherent than a brand at (4.5, 4.5, 4.5) that is materially below its targets because its strategy requires it to be at (5.0, 5.0, 5.0).

The one cross-brand comparison that is meaningful without target adjustment: Identity Clarity sub-component 1.1 (core identity). Every sub-brand should have a core identity score above 3 before significant resources are committed to Cognitive Presence or Cultural Position. A brand that does not know what it is will not convert investments in the other dimensions into equity.

Track absolute scores over time within each brand. With targets fixed (or changed only by deliberate repositioning), the absolute score trend line against a static target is the clearest signal of whether investment is working.
