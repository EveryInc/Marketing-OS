# Copywriting Line Rules (C1–C10)

Line-level copywriting rules from the sample-messaging passes. These operate below the
document-discipline rules in `references/messaging-doc-discipline.md`: those govern whether a
claim may be made and how it is sourced; these govern whether the line that carries it is
grammatical, load-bearing, and finished. Apply on every original brand line before it ships.

---

- **C1. NO IMITATION COMPRESSION.** Short-sentence, fragment-heavy "iconic" register (Apple, Palantir) is earned by a load-bearing fact under the line — a spec, a measurement, an institutional truth. Compression without a spec underneath reads as costume ("One mind. Yours." / "March remembers. June inherits."). Before shipping any compressed line, name the concrete fact it stands on; if there isn't one, write the plain claim instead.

- **C2. EVERY PRONOUN NEEDS A VISIBLE ANTECEDENT — READ THE LINE LITERALLY BEFORE SHIPPING.** "Now installed at yours" (yours what?) and "now it can run yours" (your Every?) shipped twice in one session. QC pass on every line: read it as its literal grammar with no charity. If a possessive or pronoun points at nothing, or the parallel structure breaks when expanded, the line is broken regardless of rhythm.

- **C3. THE LINE MUST SURVIVE ITS OWN LOGIC.** "Your company knows more than anyone in it. @Every is where that becomes true" — the first clause is already true, so the second is nonsense. Test each line's claim as a proposition: is it already true (twist collapses), false (claim fails), or made true/usable by the product (line works)? Only the third ships.

- **C4. GENERATE FOR A KILL RATE, NOT A FINISH LINE.** Model-drafted copy converges on competent-B2B; it does not converge on iconic with more attempts. The working division of labor: the model produces the verified message inventory and volume drafts; the human's taste performs the distillation. Never present a first pass of original brand copy as finished; present it as material with the weak lines already flagged.

- **C5. STATE THE CLAIM PLAIN BEFORE STYLING IT.** For every message, write the unstyled proposition first. Style only after the plain version is true, grammatical, and specific. If styling breaks any of the three, ship the plain version.

- **C6. THE PROMISE MUST MATCH THE ARTIFACT.** In a product example, the headline promise, user request, action taken, and result must describe the same completed job. A request that produces another prompt, plan, or recommendation does not prove that the underlying job was handled. Compression may change the words, never the causal claim.

- **C7. DO NOT TURN MARKETING INTO COMPLIANCE COPY.** Marketing makes promises. Do not weaken a line merely because it is broad, absolute, or omits edge cases. Judge a brand promise on strategic truth, ownability, memorability, and whether the product's central mechanism gives it a credible center. Keep operational claims precise: prices, permissions, security behavior, integration counts, measured outcomes, and specific capabilities still require verification. The distinction is claim type, not claim strength.

- **C8. WRITE FOR THE EAR, WITH WORDS PEOPLE USE.** Read the line aloud. Prefer common words, direct syntax, and natural speech over jargon or prestige diction. Short is a useful default, not an independent virtue: do not amputate the detail, cadence, or surprise that makes the line work, and do not enforce an arbitrary headline length.

- **C9. MAKE THE DEVICE EARN ITS PLACE.** Drama, wit, repetition, contrast, threes, twists on familiar phrases, and unexpected points of view are tools for sharpening a verified idea or recognizable observation. They are not idea generators by themselves. If removing the device reveals no proposition underneath, cut the line.

- **C10. SAY ONE THING WELL.** Give each headline, section, email, or ad one dominant communication job. Supporting copy may explain or prove it, but a second competing message weakens both. Reject "also mention" accretion unless the new material advances the same reader decision.
