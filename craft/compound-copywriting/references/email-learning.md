# Learning across an email sequence

Use these rules when a human asks to compound email edits across a campaign. They
govern writing decisions, not a fixed subject formula or sending schedule.

## Frame the benefit before the subject

- Name the gain beyond the reader's ordinary job: a new ability, a recurring failure
  avoided, work removed, or an outcome they care about. A job description is not a hook.
- Compare candidates by the change they promise, not just punchiness. A question about
  supervising a tool can make the product sound like more work.
- Do not copy a successful subject's syntax into the next email when its underlying
  promise does not fit. Preserve human-selected subjects unless reopened.
- Distinguish a broad brand promise from a measured operational claim. Do not weaken
  approved brand language into qualifications; verify the concrete evidence in the body.
- Make the preview add information: the membership benefit, a concrete use, or the
  next step. Do not spend it paraphrasing the subject.

## Make the story actionable

- Identify the person, what they asked, what the product did, and the useful output.
  Keep the detail that explains why this changes what the person can do.
- Follow the work to its consequential result when evidence supports it. An audit,
  recommendation, draft, approved change, and deployed fix are different results.
- Use named people and relevant roles as proof when available. Explain why their
  starting point matters; a person without technical training producing a code change
  is a different story from a developer doing the same task.
- Cut implementation detours and abstract morals before cutting causal detail. Retain
  how another person can use the workflow when adoption or sharing is the point.
- Prefer spoken verbs and recognizable outputs. End with a concrete next request or
  action matched to the recipient's current state.
- Retain human humor, warmth, and deliberate brand language. Shorter is useful only
  when it preserves the thought.
- Check each proposed edit against the sentences before and after it for accidental
  repetition of prominent words and their close forms. Read the revised paragraph,
  not just the replacement line. Cut or recast an echo instead of reaching for an
  awkward synonym. Preserve repetition that deliberately creates emphasis or rhythm,
  protected language, and exact terms needed for clarity.
- Do not narrate a complete example in the opening and repeat it in the first bullet.
  Let the opening establish the stakes and the examples deliver the evidence.
- Earn excitement through recognizable stakes, specific outcomes, and personality.
  Adding adjectives or implementation details does not strengthen a weak example.

## Allocate examples across the sequence

- Keep an example ledger before drafting: source, request, action, result, material
  limitations, relevant function, and every email already using it.
- Choose a deliberate set. A broad launch can demonstrate different functions; a
  themed follow-up should group related jobs. Honor the brief's count and format.
- When the human asks for fresh examples, apply that ruling across audience variants
  as well as later sends. Do not keep recycling the easiest verified trio.
- Novelty alone is insufficient. Fresh proof must remain compelling and deliver the
  subject's promise. Find stronger evidence rather than padding with unrelated tasks.
- Distinguish a coworker watching, trying, reusing a shared skill, and completing a
  job. Explain the causal link relevant to the campaign instead of attaching a generic
  claim about collaboration to every list.

## Segment before presenting

- When platform access is available, inspect saved audience definitions, exclusions,
  existing sequences, and sent reports before recommending routing or cadence. A past
  planning document is not proof that a flow is configured or effective. Do not derive
  a new flow by multiplying every membership tier by every lifecycle label.
- Confirm whether an old waitlist still represents pending access. Once access has
  been granted, distinguish people who have not installed from people who have not
  used the product; write reactivation copy, not another access announcement.
- Compare performance within relevant audience and lifecycle groups. Record send
  counts and distinguish unique clicks from total clicks and product actions. Email
  length and open-rate correlations do not establish a conversion rule. Check whether
  a proposed follow-up would duplicate an existing sequence before adding it.
- Separate membership status from product state. A paying reader can still be new to
  the product; a free reader can already be using it.
- Label each draft with its audience, entry condition, intended action, and exclusions.
  Carry these labels into review rather than presenting generic acquisition copy.
- Change the offer and CTA when entitlements or behavior differ. Do not sell a second
  membership to an existing member, promise fresh credits to an existing user, or ask
  someone to install a product they already use.
- Define precedence and exits so a person does not receive overlapping launch variants
  or a generic reminder after completing its action. Never imply draft routing is
  configured in the sending platform.
- Reuse approved proof across variants only when the brief permits it. Vary the
  opening, offer, and action for an actual audience difference, not cosmetic
  personalization. Use role targeting only when supported by known attributes or behavior.
- Choose the sender for the message. A founder's argument can use a personal voice;
  ongoing examples from multiple colleagues usually call for a team voice. Preserve
  a human-selected sender and match the reply invitation to it.
- Explain a commercial choice in human terms when that strengthens the offer. State
  pricing plainly without repeating equivalent claims or inventing unlimited access.

## Use platform evidence to shape the email

- Assign one primary reader action before choosing length or links. Acquisition earns
  installation; onboarding earns a first useful result; adoption earns repeat use;
  billing resolves an actual block. Multiple links can support one action, but do not
  add competing offers merely because an older email included them.
- For onboarding, turn a demonstration into a usable next step: the task, required
  input, a short prompt when useful, and what the reader should check afterward.
  A named practitioner's experience can establish credibility without a founder sender.
- Choose length by the decision and reader familiarity. A launch may need mechanism,
  proof, and terms; a post-install nudge may need only one task and a prompt. Do not
  turn a successful example's word count into a universal minimum or maximum.
- Treat cadence as a lifecycle decision. Immediate welcome and daily early onboarding
  are patterns to inspect, not proof that every campaign should send daily. Account
  for other live messages, entry delays, suppression, and completion before proposing
  another send. Never transplant checkout timing to product setup without evidence.
- When sequence content is editable, distinguish the current copy from the versions
  responsible for lifetime metrics. A current preview plus historical results does not
  prove that exact subject or body produced those results.
- Measure the requested behavior. A prompt meant to be used in Slack may succeed
  without an email click; an installation link click may never become an installation.
  Use opens and clicks as diagnostics alongside verified product actions when available.

## Review and compound one unit at a time

- Present the next agreed email or audience variant, incorporate the human's edits,
  and apply the underlying correction to the remaining inventory.
- Follow the requested review batch size. Do not generate an unsolicited channel as
  a transfer test; test on the next requested email or other authorized artifact.
- Preserve the prior version and canonical human edit privately. Record whether the
  lesson concerns strategy, evidence, voice, length, audience, or action.
- Treat a fresh draft as a pending transfer test until the human reviews it. Capturing
  a rule does not establish that the next draft succeeded.
- Run the requested editorial tool on every settled audience variant. A named review
  tool must not be silently replaced with a generic imitation, and its suggestions do
  not constitute the human editor's approval.
- Before replacing a shared master document, preserve its current copy and other
  sections. If editorial review must precede inclusion, review a staging draft first,
  then transfer the reviewed copy. Keep evidence gaps outside reader-facing prose and
  resolve or visibly flag them before treating the batch as ready.

## Final email check

Before presenting, check the subject's benefit, preview's contribution, example
freshness, request/action/result, adjacent-word echoes, repeated stories, segment offer,
CTA, and sender. Confirm protected language survived and operational claims have
support. Use `../evals/email-edit-regressions.json` as a compact regression checklist;
passing these checks is not evidence of conversion lift or human approval.

Keep campaign names, subjects, prices, people, and private receipts out of this reusable
reference. They belong in the campaign's private record.
