# Learning across social media

Use these rules when developing a social approach, writing a related run of posts, or
carrying human edits into the next post. They govern creative decisions. They do not
prescribe one opening, format, content mix, or posting schedule.

## Give the post a job before choosing its form

A post may explain, demonstrate, teach, entertain, provoke, or tell a story. Choose the
main job from the brief and the brand strategy. A launch post may need to explain the
product or category clearly. An always-on brand post may earn attention through humor,
taste, usefulness, commentary, or play without carrying a sales message.

Keep one main idea in view. A post may contain a joke, link, story, and video when they
all help the same thought land. Do not strip away useful context merely to satisfy a
one-line formula.

Every post needs its own reason to exist. Judge the feed separately: different posts can
build understanding, desire, familiarity, usefulness, and participation over time. The
feed's range does not excuse a weak individual post, and one post does not need to carry
the entire brand strategy.

Assign the content bucket after the idea survives editorial judgment. A bucket is a way
to organize the feed, not a premise or a publishing obligation. When a calendar row has
no sharp idea, reframe, merge, hold, or kill it before line editing; combining several
weak examples rarely creates one strong post.

## Start with the most interesting part

The strongest opening may be a product definition, category explanation, opinion, joke,
question, result, confession, number, scene, or visual. Do not turn a pattern observed in
one set of posts into a universal rule about how every post must begin.

Open with a product or category definition when the definition is itself useful: it makes
a new behavior easy to picture, gives the audience language for a change, or stakes out a
position worth repeating. Rewrite definitions that merely restate the interface, audience,
or feature inventory.

Minimal copy works when the surrounding context or creative does the explaining. Add the
missing context when an unfamiliar reader would otherwise have to decode internal lore,
an acronym, or an unsupported claim.

## Let memes be memes

A meme can earn its place through humor alone. It does not need a forced product lesson,
sales CTA, or explanation of the brand strategy. Judge it on timing, whether the audience
understands the joke, whether it is funny, and fit with the brand's established voice. A
product-free joke belongs when the intended audience finds it funny and the brand's
presence in the conversation feels natural.

Do not copy another account's character, attach the logo to a joke the brand does not
understand, or treat strong engagement as evidence of product interest. Borrow a useful
creative move, never somebody else's personality.

## Match detail to the claim

Specificity supports the idea; it is not the idea by itself. Keep details that make the
post easier to understand, believe, picture, or enjoy. Remove details that slow the joke,
repeat the creative, or inflate ordinary work.

When the point is to show what a product did, make the starting request, the product's
action, and the outcome clear. Apply that structure to demonstrations and case studies,
not to every brand post. Do not describe a task as difficult, important, or ambitious
unless the evidence and the audience's experience support that judgment.

Name the evidence or finished work directly: video, screenshot, code change, design,
guide, skill, template, customer quotation, or measured result. Avoid using “receipt,”
“artifact,” “object,” or other strategy shorthand when an ordinary noun is clearer.

## Give people a reason to pass it along

A post may help someone, amuse them, surprise them, give them words for a belief, make
them want the product, or let them express taste and identity. Decide what the reader
gets before polishing the hook. A generic question or comment prompt cannot repair a post
with no payoff.

The brand itself may make the post interesting. Do not require every execution to work
after removing its name, characters, history, or distinctive visual language. Ask whether
the post gives the audience something beyond self-congratulation and whether the larger
feed becomes more recognizable through the choice.

## Build memory without turning it into a template

Recurring characters, phrases, visual treatments, running jokes, and formats can make a
feed easier to recognize. Repeat a format while it continues to produce good ideas.
Change or retire it when the audience can predict the execution before seeing it.

Repeat the brand belief through different stories and forms. Repeating the same tagline,
example, structure, or CTA makes the idea feel smaller.

Leave room for unexpected ideas. A strong brand strategy sets boundaries; it should not
eliminate the strange idea, cultural observation, or timely joke that could only emerge
while the team is paying attention.

## Learn from edits and performance without inventing laws

Preserve the human-edited post as canonical. Classify the correction before carrying it
forward: idea, opening, voice, specificity, humor, explanation, structure, visual role,
or CTA. Apply the underlying change to the next comparable post without copying the same
syntax.

Visible engagement can identify a post worth studying. It does not establish why the
post worked or whether its voice transfers to another brand. Separate brand accounts,
founder accounts, paid distribution, giveaways, established characters, launch news,
and ordinary posts before drawing conclusions. Treat likes, replies, shares, saves,
views, traffic, and conversion as different behaviors.

Keep campaign lines, private examples, current people, and temporary platform tactics
out of this reusable reference. Save only the bounded craft decision that should change
the next social assignment.

## Final social check

- Does the post have one main job and one idea worth remembering?
- Is the opening the strongest available entry point for that job?
- Does the reader receive something useful, funny, surprising, desirable, or worth
  repeating?
- Is explanation present where it helps and absent where the creative already lands?
- If the post shows product work, are the request, action, and outcome understandable?
- Does the post sound like this brand rather than a borrowed social personality?
- Does the post stand on its own, and does it add something useful to the larger feed?

Passing this check is an editorial judgment, not evidence that the post will perform.
