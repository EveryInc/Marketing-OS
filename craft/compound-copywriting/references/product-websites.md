# Product Website Copy

Use this reference for iterative homepages, workflow libraries, integrations pages, and
pricing pages. It captures the reusable operations behind a heavily edited product-site
pass. The live human-edited document remains canonical for its copy, structure, formatting,
and protected language. Verified GTM, positioning, product, security, pricing, and integration
sources remain authoritative for factual claims.

## Start from ruled copy

1. Read the current document, the applicable positioning and GTM source, and any accepted
   prior drafts before generating alternatives.
2. Rank language by authority: explicitly approved or restored; current human-edited copy;
   verified source copy; working draft; discarded draft.
3. Protect any line the human selected, restored, repeated, or defended. A request to edit
   another section does not reopen it.
4. Search ruled drafts for strong unused language before writing net-new copy. Recover the
   line or the operation that made it strong; do not imitate its surface rhythm elsewhere.
5. Keep unresolved pricing, security, permission, measured-result, and capability claims
   visibly blocked. Build the structural slot without filling it with plausible prose.

## Give every section one conversion job

Map the page before polishing it. Every section must advance one reader decision or answer
one objection. Common jobs are:

- Understand the promise.
- Believe the product can do useful work.
- Understand the smallest path to first use.
- See the distinctive mechanism.
- Recognize relevant outcomes.
- Recognize oneself as the buyer or internal champion.
- Resolve trust, control, or price.
- Act.

If two sections do the same job, merge, cut, or reassign one. Do not keep both by rewriting
the same claim with new nouns. Repetition earns its place when it creates progression, such
as workflow to skill to company adoption, or when a CTA recurs at a new decision point.

## Make the hero choose

- Lead with one sharp promise or tension. The hero cannot carry the entire message map.
- Put the product in a familiar category with plain language, then show the distinctive
  behavior or consequence.
- Prefer a visible human behavior over a generic benefit stack. “Show the team” is stronger
  than a paragraph claiming efficiency, learning, collaboration, and transformation at once.
- Write the mechanism in actions a user recognizes. Avoid generated product diction such as
  “hand it a task,” “ask for a finished result,” “jobs piling up,” or “the result lands.”
  These phrases describe an agent from the outside. Use the interaction itself: tag it with
  a job, correct the work, run it again, open the pull request, or place the order.
- Read the hero aloud. If it sounds like a product marketer explaining software rather than
  a person describing what they want, rewrite it.

## Put receipts where belief is the next problem

Concrete internal case studies can belong directly below the hero when the unfamiliar
product's next question is “Can it do anything useful?” Use the strongest completed jobs.
Do not replace product receipts with customer logos when that section's job is proof of work.

Build a proof inventory before drafting:

| Example | Request | Action | Completed result | Approved source | Page placement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Rules:

- Use only audited, approved workflows for public proof.
- Do not invent a plausible use case to fill a layout.
- Do not repeat the hero's top receipts in a later breadth grid unless the repetition serves
  a new decision. Reserve new approved examples for breadth.
- If customer examples are pending, leave explicit beta slots. Do not disguise internal
  examples as customer proof.
- Prefer a small number of complete receipts over a large number of thin claims.

## Keep transformation cards honest

A transformation headline may compress the story, but the request, action, and result must
prove the same completed job.

For “Turn X into Y” cards:

1. **X** names the actual starting material or state.
2. **Y** names a concrete completed artifact, decision, or change.
3. The user's ask requests an action. A status report such as “my email was hacked” is not a
   product request.
4. The action taken bridges X and Y without a hidden human step.
5. The result names what was created, changed, sent, opened, ordered, scheduled, or shipped.

A prompt that produces another prompt, a plan for later work, or generic advice does not
prove that the underlying job was done. Narrow the headline or replace the example.

When a repeated card set has a human-edited exemplar, treat it as a schema. Preserve:

- headline grammar and transformation format;
- label names and field order;
- the requested number of items;
- prompt voice and punctuation;
- role, connection, context, and final-work distinctions;
- intentional native formatting, including highlights and emphasis.

Apply the pattern to the remaining cards without copying their subject matter.

## Explain the mechanism after earning attention

Keep “how it works” literal and short. Use the minimum steps required to reach the first
valuable behavior. Name the interface action, what the product does, and what the human does
next. Do not turn setup instructions into a manifesto.

Give a distinctive owned mechanism its own section when it explains why the product improves
or why the company changes. Show the loop through observable actions. Do not restate the hero
or claim unverified retention, memory, or performance.

When the buyer is an internal champion, speak to the social job: proving the new behavior to
the rest of the company. Recover strong owned language for this section before writing new
copy. A line that explains distribution inside the organization can do more work than another
paragraph about collaboration.

## Model competitors at the right altitude

Use reference sites to answer structural questions:

- What must the reader understand before scrolling?
- Where does concrete proof appear?
- How is breadth merchandised?
- Which objections get their own section?
- What is the shortest path to action?

Borrow the conversion logic, not the competitor's sentence shape, copy density, feature list,
or number of sections. A reference page can justify a directory, a searchable workflow grid,
or a request form; it cannot justify keeping content that performs no job on this product's
page.

## Separate brand promises from operational claims

Marketing is allowed to make strong claims. Preserve a bold brand promise when it is ownable,
memorable, directionally true, and grounded in the product's central mechanism. Do not add
edge-case qualifications that turn the hero into compliance copy.

Verify operational claims precisely: prices, credits, integration counts, permissions,
security behavior, retention, autonomous actions, measured improvement, and specific product
capabilities. When approval is pending, keep the public slot blocked.

## Finish the conversion path

- Repeat one primary CTA at the moments where a new reader decision has been resolved.
- End substantial secondary pages with a CTA appropriate to what the reader just learned.
- Treat exact counts, requested list lengths, field order, and link destinations as copy
  requirements, not formatting suggestions.
- If the goal is activation, judge tests by activated workspaces or the relevant downstream
  behavior. Do not declare a headline winner on clicks alone.
- Run a final duplication audit, proof-allocation audit, blocked-claim audit, spoken-language
  pass, and native-format comparison before approval.
