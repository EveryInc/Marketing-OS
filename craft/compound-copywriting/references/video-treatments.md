# Video Treatments

Use this reference for launch films, product videos, brand films, and other scripted
marketing videos. A treatment is a production document, not a prose summary of the idea.
It must let a reviewer judge the story, spoken copy, product proof, visual action, humor,
and runtime before the shoot.

## Start from one engine

State the strategic tension in plain language, then turn it into one story or comic
engine. The engine should create the scenes rather than sit above them as explanation.

Examples of engines:

- A recognized expert discovers that telling coworkers about a new behavior did not
  change how they worked; a shared product makes the behavior spread.
- A repeated human bottleneck becomes the setup, callback, and eventual product payoff.
- One person's successful use becomes the next person's starting point.

Do not build a hero film as a list of use cases joined by voice-over. Each beat should
advance the plot, reveal character, demonstrate the product, or pay off an earlier setup.
The strongest beats do more than one.

## Write the shootable treatment

Use a timed beat table with these fields:

| TIME / BEAT | SCRIPT | PICTURE / ACTION | NOTES |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact range and beat name | Every spoken line and voice-over | What the viewer sees happen | Supers, source footage, sound, production constraints, and open questions |

Keep the columns distinct:

- **Script** is what the audience hears.
- **Picture / action** is what changes on screen. Do not repeat the script as a visual
  description.
- **Notes** carry supers, product-capture requirements, legal language, sound, and
  production facts. Parentheticals and supers are part of the writing when they add
  character, context, or a second joke.

An approval-deck mini-treatment may summarize the premise and mandate. Once the concept
is approved, replace that summary with the timed production treatment. Do not present a
paragraph as a finished treatment.

## Put proof inside the scene

Every product beat must show the causal chain:

1. A specific person makes a specific request.
2. The product takes visible action.
3. The audience sees the completed result or changed state.
4. When the product compounds, a later person starts from the earlier person's work,
   correction, or reusable method.

The dialogue may carry the joke. The picture must carry the receipt. Show the request,
corrections, finished output, handoff, saved method, approval, or other artifact that
proves the claim. Do not pause the story for a generic feature demonstration.

## Use character-based humor

Humor should come from recognizable behavior, a true product tension, or a character's
specific relationship to the work. Distribute it across:

- Spoken lines.
- Behavior and location.
- Supers and titles.
- Repetition, callbacks, and escalation.

Do not add interchangeable jokes after the treatment is written. A joke earns its place
when it reveals character, advances the story, sharpens the product mechanism, or pays
off a setup. Preserve odd specifics, parentheticals, fragments, and visual asides selected
by the human; smoothing them often removes the voice.

## Make the strategy visible

Do not rely on the closing voice-over to explain the strategic idea. If the promise is
that a behavior spreads through a company, the sequence must show one person's use
becoming another person's ability. If the promise is that corrections compound, show the
correction changing the next job. The audience should understand the mechanism before
the closing line names it.

## Time honestly

Read every spoken line aloud at performance pace and run a table read before locking the
timecodes. Account for pauses, reaction shots, product comprehension, supers, and the end
card. A dense paragraph cannot occupy a two-second beat merely because the table says it
does.

When the script runs long, protect the engine, setup, causal proof, callback, and CTA.
Cut repeated explanation and redundant examples before cutting the detail that makes a
scene intelligible or funny.

## Treatment check

- [ ] One story or comic engine governs the film
- [ ] The treatment uses time, script, picture/action, and notes as separate fields
- [ ] Every beat advances the story or earns its runtime another way
- [ ] Product claims are proven through visible request, action, and result
- [ ] The strategic mechanism happens on screen before it is explained
- [ ] Recurring jokes have a setup, escalation, and payoff
- [ ] Supers add meaning or humor instead of repeating the dialogue
- [ ] A read-through confirms that the script fits the stated runtime
- [ ] The CTA and operational offer match the current approved source
