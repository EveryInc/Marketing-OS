# Design Lineage

The designers Every's art direction is measured against. Ruled by Douglas, Nov. 8, 2026:
art direction rests on Bruno Munari first, then George Lois, Paul Rand, Saul Bass, Milton Glaser, Ivan
Chermayeff (with Chermayeff & Geismar), and the Swiss school where the surface calls for
it. Dieter Rams's principles sit beside Munari as the review standard.

This file turns each into a working rule: what to take, when to use it, and the test it
applies. It is not a style menu. Every borrows the reasoning, not the look. A layout that
quotes a Bass poster or a Swiss grid without the reasoning is pastiche and fails
`reference-doctrine.md`.

Full entries live in `../../../foundation/marketing-os/references/canon.md`. The lenses
behind them (semiotics, ways of seeing, design as art) live in
`../../../foundation/marketing-os/references/groundings.md`.

## Bruno Munari: the spine

Munari carries the most weight because he is the source of both the attitude and the
method.

**The attitude** (*Design as Art*, 1966). The designer is an artist who has come down off
the pedestal to work inside everyday life. There is no split between beautiful things to
look at and ugly things to use. Designers should explain their methods simply, so people
can see the care in the object. Art direction at Every serves use and understanding;
decoration that does neither is cut.

**The method** (*Da cosa nasce cosa*, 1981). Munari's design process runs in order:

1. Define the problem.
2. Break it into its elements.
3. Collect data, including what others have already made.
4. Analyze the data.
5. Creativity: the idea, kept inside the limits the analysis found.
6. Materials and technologies available.
7. Experimentation.
8. Models.
9. Verification with real users and real contexts.
10. Construction drawings, then the solution.

**How Every uses the method.** It is the order of operations for new identity and campaign
visual work, and it maps onto this skill:

| Munari step | This skill |
|---|---|
| Problem, elements | The brief, positioning, and the job of each surface |
| Data collection and analysis | Human reference gate and `competitive-semiotic-audit.md` |
| Creativity | No more than two territories |
| Materials and technologies | `agent-readable-production.md`: tools, fonts, components, renderers |
| Experimentation, models | Representative surfaces and generative branches |
| Verification | `design-review.md` and real-context checks (feed, phone, stage, inbox) |

Creativity comes fifth, not first. Do not present a territory before the problem, its
elements, and the field have been worked through.

**Munari's play.** His useless machines, unreadable books, and children's books show that
rigor and play are compatible. When Every needs wit in form, the model is Munari's: a
playful object that still explains itself, never whimsy that hides the use.

**Tests.**
- Can you state the problem and its elements before showing a visual?
- Does the form explain what the thing is and how to use it?
- Would a first-time viewer see the care in the object without being told?

## Dieter Rams: the review standard

Good design is innovative, useful, aesthetic, understandable, unobtrusive, honest,
long-lasting, thorough down to the last detail, environmentally friendly, and as little
design as possible. "Less, but better." Run these in `design-review.md` before handoff.

## Paul Rand: the mark

Rand's IBM work and his writing (*Thoughts on Design*, 1947; "Logos, Flags, and
Escutcheons," AIGA, 1991) set the rules for marks:

- A logo identifies; it does not sell directly or describe the business.
- A logo gets its meaning from the quality of the thing it stands for, not the other way
  around. A mark cannot rescue a weak product or a vague position.
- The designer unifies, simplifies, and removes what is superfluous. Ideas need not be
  obscure to be original.

**Use when** making or revising a mark, a sub-brand lockup, a stamp, or an icon system,
including Every's variable E and product marks.

**Tests.** Does the mark work at favicon size and in one color? Is anyone expecting it to
explain the product? (It should not have to.) Is the position strong enough to give the
mark meaning?

## Saul Bass: reduction and motion

Bass made the title sequence a designed piece of storytelling (*The Man with the Golden
Arm*, 1955; *Vertigo*, *North by Northwest*, *Psycho* with Hitchcock) and built corporate
marks that held for decades (AT&T's bell in 1969 and globe in 1983, Continental's
jetstream in 1968, United's tulip in 1974). He described the work as serving the film.

- Reduce a story to one symbol that can carry it.
- Make the symbol move. Typography and form can animate the idea, not just decorate the
  opening.
- Serve the content. The open sets up the thing it introduces.

**Use when** designing video opens, launch films, every.to/tv idents, social motion,
event screens, and any mark that must also animate.

**Tests.** Can the core image be drawn in a few strokes? Does the motion carry meaning, or
only movement? Does the open serve what follows it?

## Milton Glaser: wit, drawing, and the city

Glaser co-founded Push Pin Studios (1954) and *New York* magazine with Clay Felker (1968),
made the Bob Dylan poster (1967) and I ♥ NY (1977), and wrote *Art Is Work* (2000). He moved
between illustration, type, editorial, identity, and civic design.

- A mark can be a sentence people complete themselves. I ♥ NY is a rebus the reader
  finishes, which is why it was copied endlessly and still points home.
- Drawing and a personal hand belong in commercial work.
- Editorial design is brand design. A magazine's look is its point of view.

**Use when** Every's work must be warm, witty, or civic; when a mark should invite
participation; when designing editorial surfaces for every.to, Thesis, or newsletters.

**Tests.** Does the reader complete it? Would someone redraw or remix it on their own and
still credit Every? (See memetics in `groundings.md`.)

## George Lois: the big idea in one image

Lois art-directed Esquire's covers from 1962 to 1972, including Muhammad Ali posed as
Saint Sebastian, and helped launch MTV with "I Want My MTV." With Glenn O'Brien, he is
Douglas's closest creative reference.

- One image or line carries the whole idea. Everything else supports it.
- A cover, ad or poster can make an argument about the culture, not only announce a
  product.
- Provocation is earned by the product's real virtue, not added for attention.

**Use when** making campaign key art, covers, launch images, and any piece that has to
work at a glance.

**Tests.** Can you say the idea in one sentence and show it in one image? Does the
provocation point back to something true about the product?

## Ivan Chermayeff and Chermayeff & Geismar: systems, modernizing, collage

Founded 1957 with Tom Geismar (now Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv). The firm's marks include
Chase, Mobil, Pan Am, NBC's peacock (modernized in 1986), Showtime, National Geographic,
the Smithsonian (1998), the Museum of Modern Art, the Library of Congress, and
HarperCollins. Chermayeff also made found-object collages with a dry sense of humor.

- Bold abstraction, primary color, and strong letterforms simplify a crowded mark.
- Modernizing a worn mark is as important as making a new one. Keep what people already
  recognize and remove the rest (the NBC peacock lost five feathers, not its identity).
- Collage and found material can carry wit in a modernist system.

**Use when** refreshing a sub-brand, consolidating competing marks, or building collage-led campaign imagery.

**Tests.** What recognition must survive the refresh? Is the simplified mark still
recognizably the old one to existing customers? Does the collage have a single idea?

## The Swiss school, where applicable

The International Typographic Style: Ernst Keller's teaching from 1918; Armin Hofmann and
Emil Ruder at the Basel School of Design from 1947; Josef Müller-Brockmann on the grid and
Akzidenz-Grotesk, co-editing *Neue Grafik*; Max Bill; and the sans serifs that came with it
(Akzidenz-Grotesk, 1896; Univers and Helvetica, 1957). Its principles are cleanliness,
readability, objectivity, a mathematical grid, asymmetric layout, sans serif type, and
photography over illustration.

**Where it applies.** Information-heavy and system surfaces: the Skills Library, product
documentation, pricing, data visuals, dashboards, event wayfinding, schedules, and design
system components. Every's Founders Grotesk and Mono already sit in this lineage.

**Where it does not.** Campaign moments that need warmth, wit, or a personal hand. There,
Glaser, Bass, or Munari's play leads, and the grid stays underneath.

**Tests.** Is there a grid, and does every element sit on it? Can the reader find the
information without reading the decoration? If the surface is emotional, has the grid been
allowed to recede?

## When the lineage disagrees

- **Swiss objectivity vs Glaser's hand.** Use the grid for information and the hand for
  feeling. A campaign can put a drawn element on a strict grid.
- **Rand's "a logo does not explain" vs Glaser's rebus.** The primary mark identifies. A
  campaign mark can invite participation.
- **Rams's "as little design as possible" vs Munari's play.** Restraint is the default;
  play must still explain the object.
